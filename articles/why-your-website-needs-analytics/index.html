Why Your Website Needs Analytics
and Which Numbers Matter

Ask a small business owner how their website is doing and you tend to get one of two answers. "Fine, I think." Or a visitor count, quoted with the shrug of someone who already suspects it doesn't mean much. Both come from the same place: the data exists, but nobody ever said which part to look at.

That's a shame. A flyer through a letterbox is a guess; your website knows how someone found you, which page convinced them, and whether they picked up the phone. You just have to ask it the right four questions.

Why the visitor count helps you least

Almost every conversation about website statistics starts with visitors per month. It is the easiest number to find and the one that moves most, so it feels like the important one.

It isn't. Two hundred visitors a month who live within fifteen kilometres and want exactly what you do are worth more than two thousand from a blog post that went briefly viral abroad. A hairdresser whose visitor numbers doubled after a national article had exactly as many extra customers in the chair: none.

Visitor counts only mean something next to the answers to two other questions: who arrived, and what did they do. Those are the numbers below.

1 Where your visitors come from

Your traffic source report splits visits into a handful of buckets: Google search, your Google Business Profile or Maps, direct, social, and referrals from other sites.

This is the number that decides where your time goes. If two-thirds of your visits come in through your Google Business Profile, then an hour adding photos and answering reviews there is worth more than an hour rewriting your homepage. If almost nothing comes from search, either you aren't findable for the terms your customers use, or your pages aren't about those terms at all.

A large, steady direct share is worth noting too: usually returning customers and word of mouth — healthy, but a sign you're not winning many new people through search.

2 Which pages actually do the work

Every site has pages that carry the business and pages nobody has opened since launch. You almost never guess which is which.

Look at two things: which pages get the most visits, and which pages people arrive on. Those are different reports and the second is the interesting one. It is common for a trade business to discover that four out of five new visitors land on one specific service page, never the homepage, because that page matches what people search for.

Then the work becomes obvious. That page gets your best photos, a real price signal, the review from a customer with the same job, and a button visible without scrolling. The page about your company history, good for eleven visits a month, does not.

3 How many people take the next step

This is the number most small business sites never set up, and without it the other three are just gossip. A "next step" is whatever counts as a lead for you: a tapped phone number, a submitted contact form, a WhatsApp click, a route request, a booking.

Every serious analytics tool can register these as events, and it takes a developer maybe twenty minutes. The result is one number you can put next to your visits: 340 visits, 22 next steps. That ratio — call it six or seven percent — is now something you can move.

Once it is measurable, ordinary changes stop being matters of taste. You move the phone number into the mobile header and the ratio goes from four percent to seven. That is no longer an opinion about design; it is roughly one extra enquiry a week.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Installing analytics and never defining a single conversion. The tool then dutifully records visits, sessions, bounce rates and average time on page for two years, and you learn nothing from any of it. If you set up only one thing this month, make it "phone number tapped" and "form submitted". Everything else can wait.

4 What your site costs people in waiting

The fourth number doesn't come from your analytics tool but from Google Search Console, and it is free: how fast your site loads for real visitors on real devices, on the connection they actually have.

That says more than a one-off speed test, which runs on a fast machine in a data centre while your customer is on a phone with two bars in a car park. Google uses this same field data as a ranking signal, so a slow page costs you twice: fewer people find it, and more of the ones who do give up before it appears.

You don't need to become an expert. You need to notice when a page turns red, and know it almost always traces back to one thing: an image somebody uploaded straight off a camera.

Privacy-friendly tools, and what you can skip

Google Analytics is free and powerful, but for a local business also enormous, and under the GDPR it puts you in cookie-banner territory with a consent notice most visitors dismiss — so the data you get back is incomplete anyway.

The lighter alternatives — Plausible, Fathom, Simple Analytics, a self-hosted Matomo — cost a few euros a month, set no cookies, don't track people across sites, and generally need no banner. Their dashboards fit on one screen, which here is a feature, and they load a fraction of the weight, which helps number four.

  • Google Search Console — free. Shows which search terms you appear for and your speed on real visits.
  • One lightweight analytics tool — sources, landing pages, conversions. One screen is enough.
  • Two or three events — phone tap, form submission, route or booking click.
  • Your Google Business Profile stats — calls, route requests and clicks through to your site, already in the profile for free.

Skip the rest: heatmaps and session recordings need more traffic than a few hundred visits a month to show a pattern, and nobody opens the PDF a dashboard emails them.

The ten-minute monthly routine

Numbers are only worth collecting if somebody looks at them, and the biggest reason people stop looking is checking daily. Daily figures for a local business are noise: one quiet Tuesday means nothing. Once a month, do this.

  1. Next steps first — calls, forms and route clicks versus last month. The only number that pays bills.
  2. Then the sources — did anything shift? A drop in search is worth investigating; a drop in social usually means you didn't post.
  3. Then the landing pages — has a new page started pulling people in? Give that one attention.
  4. Then Search Console — search terms you appear for but hadn't thought of are free page ideas. Any page gone red on speed?
  5. Write down one thing to change — one, done before next month. Ten small fixes a year beat one big redesign.

Conclusion

Analytics for a small business isn't about dashboards. It is four questions: where people came from, which page caught them, how many took the next step, and how long they waited for the page to appear.

Set up conversion tracking this week — even just the phone number and the contact form — and give it a month. That one number, next to your visits, will tell you more than any report you have read so far.
