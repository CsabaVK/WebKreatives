Web Design Tips

Accessibility Basics
for Small Websites

Csaba Garaguly
· 22 September 2026 · 7 min read

Ask a small business owner about accessibility and you tend to get one of two reactions: a slightly worried look ("do we need a lawsuit-proof website?"), or a shrug ("that's for banks and government sites, not us"). Both miss what's actually going on. Accessibility on a website isn't a legal category or a badge you earn. It's a list of real people who currently can't use your site properly — and every one of them could be a customer.

None of it requires a redesign or a developer with special training. The fixes that matter most for a small business are small: contrast, text size, alt text, how your site responds to a keyboard, and what your links actually say. Get those right and you've covered the vast majority of real-world friction, without ever opening a compliance checklist.

The mistake that keeps it off the to-do list

Most small businesses that do think about accessibility make the same mistake: they treat it as a one-time purchase. They install an overlay widget — the little icon in the corner that promises to fix everything automatically — tick the box mentally, and move on. Overlays can resize text and boost contrast on request, but they can't add a label to a form field that never had one, describe an image that has no alt text, or make a menu open with a keyboard. Visitors who actually rely on assistive technology often find these widgets get in the way rather than help. Accessibility isn't something you bolt on afterwards. It's a handful of habits you build in while you're building the page.

Who actually benefits

"Accessibility" sounds like it's about a small group of people using specialist software. In practice, most of the people it helps are your regular customers, just having a bad five minutes.

Scenario 1

Checking your hours in sunlight

Pale grey text on a white background looks fine on your desktop monitor indoors. On a phone screen in direct sun, it disappears entirely.

Scenario 2

Reading with older eyes

A large share of your customers are over fifty. Small body text and low contrast cost you readers before they even reach your offer.

Scenario 3

Booking with one hand

A sling, a sprained wrist, a toddler on the other arm. Suddenly a mouse is awkward and the keyboard — or a thumb — is doing all the work.

None of these people identify as needing "accessible" design. They just quietly leave when a site doesn't cooperate, and you never find out why.

Contrast: the cheapest fix with the biggest payoff

Light grey text on a white or pale background is everywhere in web design right now, because it looks calm and modern on a big, well-lit screen. Out in the real world — a phone at arm's length, a shop counter, an overcast afternoon — it's one of the most common reasons people simply can't read a site.

You don't need to learn contrast ratios to fix this. Open your homepage on your phone, step outside, and read it in normal daylight without squinting. Do the same for every button: text on a coloured button needs to be genuinely dark-on-light or light-on-dark, not a stylish middle grey. If you have to concentrate to read your own price or your own phone number, so does everyone else.

If you have to squint to read your own homepage on your phone in daylight, so does everyone else — they just won't tell you.

Text size and how people actually read on a phone

Keep body text at 16 pixels or larger, and let it scale — never lock it to a fixed size that ignores a visitor's browser settings. Plenty of people turn their phone's text size up a notch or two as a permanent setting, and a site that fights that adjustment feels broken to them, even though nothing is technically wrong.

Line length matters just as much as size. Paragraphs that stretch edge to edge on a wide screen are exhausting to track line by line; capping your content column keeps sentences at a readable width automatically. And leave real space between lines — text packed tight is harder for everyone to follow, not just people with low vision.

Alt text that actually describes something

Alt text is the short description attached to an image, read aloud by screen readers and shown if the image fails to load. Most small business sites either skip it or fill it with the filename. Neither helps anyone, and neither helps Google understand the image either.

Write it the way you'd describe the photo to someone on the phone who can't see it: "Barista pouring latte art into a white cup" beats "image", and it beats "coffee shop" too — be specific about what's actually in the frame. Purely decorative images (a background texture, a divider) can have empty alt text on purpose, so a screen reader skips over them instead of reading out noise.

The keyboard test almost nobody runs

Put your mouse out of reach for two minutes. Load your homepage and press Tab repeatedly. Can you see, at every step, which link or button is currently selected? Can you reach your menu, open it, and get into your contact form using only Tab, Shift+Tab and Enter?

On a surprising number of small business sites, the answer is no. The focus outline has been switched off for looking "cleaner," or a custom dropdown menu only responds to a mouse hover. That doesn't just affect people who can't use a mouse at all — it affects anyone briefly without one: a laptop trackpad that's misbehaving, a wireless mouse with a dead battery, a customer filling in your form one-handed.

Stop writing "click here"

Screen reader users often scan a page by pulling up a list of every link on it, out of context, the way you might skim a table of contents. A page full of "click here," "read more" and "learn more" gives that list zero information. A page where every link says what it actually leads to — "see our pricing," "download the brochure," "book a free call" — works for that visitor, and it happens to read better for everyone else scanning quickly too.

None of these five fixes need a developer or a redesign. Here's the ten-minute version you can run on your own site right now:

  • Read your homepage on your phone, outside, without squinting.
  • Zoom your browser to 200% and check nothing overlaps or gets cut off.
  • Read every alt text on your homepage out loud — does it describe the image?
  • Unplug the mouse and tab through your own contact form, start to finish.
  • Search your site's pages for "click here" and "read more," and rename them.

Conclusion

Accessibility for a small business site was never really about compliance — it's about how many of the people already searching for you can actually use what they find. Contrast, text size, alt text, keyboard access and honest link wording cost nothing to fix and rule out none of your real customers.

Pick one from the list above and check it today. The gaps are usually small, and closing them tends to make the whole site feel calmer and clearer for every visitor, not just the ones you were worried about.
