How to Photograph Your Own
Business With Just a Phone

Csaba Garaguly
· 18 September 2026 · 7 min read

Open the websites of ten businesses in your own street and you can tell within a second which ones took their own photos. Not because the homemade ones are sharper — often they are not. You can tell because the real ones show the actual counter, the actual hands, the actual bread. The others show a model laughing in a kitchen that exists only in a stock library somewhere in Colorado.

Visitors read that difference immediately, even if they could not name it. They are not grading your photography. They are checking whether the place is real, whether it looks like somewhere they would walk into, and whether the people in the pictures are the people who will pick up the phone.

The good news is that the phone in your pocket is more than enough. The gap between a phone photo that earns trust and one that quietly costs you a customer is almost never the camera. It is light, height and clutter — and all three are free.

Here is how one focused hour usually breaks down.

  1. Pass 1 — the space, 15 minutes. The front, the door, the room a customer sees first. Tidy first, shoot second.
  2. Pass 2 — the work, 20 minutes. Hands doing the thing you get paid for. Mid-job, not posed at the end.
  3. Pass 3 — the people, 15 minutes. You and anyone a customer will actually meet, in the place where they will meet you.
  4. Pass 4 — the details, 10 minutes. The tools, the products, the small things that say this is a real business with a real standard.

1 Light does almost all of the work

Every phone photo that looks cheap has the same cause: the light was wrong and the phone tried to rescue it. Indoors under ceiling spots, the camera pushes the sensitivity up, colours go orange and grainy, and faces get a hard shadow under the nose.

The fix is to shoot where daylight already is. Stand your subject near a window, side-on to it rather than in front of it, and turn the ceiling lights off — mixing daylight with warm bulbs is what produces those muddy, two-coloured photos. If you have to shoot outdoors, avoid hard midday sun; an overcast day is a giant softbox and costs nothing.

One more habit worth building: never shoot a person with a bright window behind them. Your eyes compensate for backlight, your phone does not, and you end up with a silhouette in front of a blown-out white rectangle.

2 Shoot at the height of the subject, not your eyes

The single fastest improvement most business photos can make is that the photographer crouches. Phone snaps look like phone snaps largely because they are all taken from standing height, looking down — which flattens the room and makes everything on a table look small and far away.

Put the camera at the height of whatever matters. A plate on a table gets shot from plate height, or straight down from directly above. A treatment chair gets shot from seated height, because that is the eye line of the customer sitting in it. A shop floor gets shot from just below eye level, which makes the ceiling feel higher and the space feel larger.

Two settings help here. Turn on the grid in your camera app and put the important thing on a line rather than dead centre. And tap the screen on your subject before you shoot — that tells the phone what to expose and focus for, instead of averaging the whole frame.

3 The shot list a local business actually needs

Most people shoot the wrong things. They photograph the finished product from twelve angles and then have nothing to put on the about page or the contact page. Work through a list instead, and stop when it is done.

  • The entrance — what the customer sees as they arrive, so they recognise it in the street. This one photo prevents more missed appointments than any map.
  • One wide shot of the space — clean, empty, shot from a corner. It answers "what am I walking into".
  • Three or four work shots — hands mid-job. Cutting, wiring, mixing, adjusting, explaining. Movement reads as competence; a posed pair of crossed arms does not.
  • A portrait of every person a customer meets — at the window, half-turned, looking at the camera. Not the office corridor, not the company polo shirt against a white wall.
  • Two or three detail shots — the tools, the labels, the finish. These fill the gaps in a layout and they are the easiest photos to take well.
  • One before-and-after pair — from the same spot and roughly the same height. For anything that changes a customer's house or hair, this is the most persuasive image you own.

Shoot each of those a handful of times rather than once. Storage is free, and a second attempt from ten centimetres lower is usually the one you keep.

4 What should never reach the website

Tidying is photography. Ninety percent of what makes a business photo feel amateur is not technique, it is the stuff in the frame: a bin bag by the door, a cable across the floor, a supplier's cardboard box, last year's poster curling off the wall, a screen showing a half-finished spreadsheet.

Before each pass, look at the frame and remove everything that is not part of the story. Then look again — there is always one more thing.

Common mistake

Shooting in portrait only. Websites are wide, and a tall photo cropped to a wide banner loses the top and bottom of whatever you were photographing. Take the important ones in landscape as well, even if the whole rest of your life is vertical.

Also leave out: photos with a date stamp in the corner, screenshots of photos, anything a customer is recognisable in without having said yes, and the flash. Especially the flash. A phone flash makes a shiny forehead, a hard black shadow behind the subject and a room that looks like a police report.

5 Editing: three adjustments, then stop

You do not need an app with filters. Your phone's built-in editor has everything that matters, and the goal is to make the photo look like the room did, not like a mood board.

  1. Straighten and crop. A level horizon and a door frame that is actually vertical do more for the professional feel of a photo than any filter. Crop to the story, and crop wide.
  2. Lift exposure and shadows slightly. Indoor photos are almost always a touch dark. Small moves — enough that you can see into the corners, not enough to grey out the image.
  3. Fix the white balance. If the photo is orange, cool it until white things look white. This is the adjustment that most often separates a photo that looks taken from a photo that looks found.

Then leave it alone. Apply the same three moves across the whole set so the photos look like a family, and skip the heavy presets entirely — a consistent, slightly plain set beats one dramatic image next to nine ordinary ones.

Last practical point: send the photos to whoever builds your site at full size, straight from the phone. Do not send them through a chat app that compresses them to a quarter of their resolution. Any decent build will compress them properly for the web anyway, and it can only do that well if it starts from the original.

Conclusion

You do not need a photographer to have honest photos of your business. You need an hour, a window, a tidy frame, and the discipline to crouch. Work the four passes, follow the shot list, and make the three edits.

Pick the hour this week when the light in your place is best — most people already know when that is — and start with the entrance. One real photo of your own front door is worth more than a gallery of strangers smiling in someone else's premises.
